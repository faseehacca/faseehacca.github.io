### Listed Company

A company that has its shares listed on the stock exchange or **quoted or floated** on a stock exchange.
- Directors
	- Agents
	- Run the company
	- Remuneration (salary, bonuses or perks) maximization
- Shareholders
	- Principle
	- Own the company and provide funds
	- Returns maximization

---
### Agency Conflict between Shareholders and Directors

Both objectives (directors' objective and shareholders' objective) are conflicting to each other. For example, if shareholders want to maximize the returns, they have to earn more profits and cut down enough costs. One of those costs is directors' salaries or bonuses.
However, directors owe a fiduciary duty to the shareholders, which puts them in a position of loyalty and care to put shareholders' needs before themselves. The conflict of objectives leads to a mistrust between the shareholders and the directors. 

---
### Financial Statements and Need for Audit

Directors use an Annual Reports as a medium to report on the company's performance. There are five financial statements:

1. Statement of Financial Position
2. Statement of Profit and Loss and other Comprehensive Income
3. Statement of Changes in Equity
4. Statement of Cashflows
5. Notes to the Accounts / Disclosure Notes

Shareholders have to rely on these financial statements in order to assess the performance of the company. Now, since there is a conflicting objective, shareholders do not trust the directors fully to tell the truth in the financial statements.
Financial statements reflect on the directors' performance, hence, directors may try to manipulate financial statements by:

- Overstating assets and revenue
- Understating liabilities and expenses
- Giving inadequate disclosures

For these reasons, shareholders want a Reasonable Assurance that financial statements are reliable and accurate, that's why Assurance Engagements and Audit are needed.

### Assurance Engagement

An engagement in which a practitioner expresses a conclusion designed to enhance the degree of confidence of the intended users, other than the responsible party, about the outcome of the evaluation or measurement of a subject matter against criteria.

---
### Elements of Assurance Engagement

An external audit must include the five elements of an assurance engagement:

1. Appropriate subject matter
2. A three-party relationship
3. Suitable criteria
4. Sufficient appropriate evidence
5. A written assurance report (independent's auditor's report)

---
### Appropriate subject matter

Data prepared or assertions made by the responsible party.  
The subject matter can take many forms, such as:

- Financial performance
- Non-financial performance, for example the key indicators of efficiency and effectiveness
- Physical characteristics, for example, the capacity of a facility
- Systems and processes, for example, an entity's internal control or IT system
- Behavior, for example, corporate governance, compliance with regulation

---
### Three-Party Relationship

A three-party relationship includes:

- A responsible party (the directors) - the person responsible for information and assertions
- A practitioner (external auditor) - The practitioner is responsible for determining the nature, timing and extent of procedures and is required to pursue anything that leads the practitioner to question whether the subject matter information should be changed in some material respect.
- Intended users (shareholders) - are the people for whom the practitioner prepares the assurance report. The responsible party can be one of the intended users.

---
### Criteria

Criteria are the benchmarks used to evaluate or measure the subject matter.  
Examples are:

- **Financial statements** → IFRS.
- **Internal controls** → Internal control framework.
- **Compliance** → Applicable laws, regulations, or contracts

Without suitable criteria, conclusions may be subjective, inconsistent, and open to misunderstanding.

**Characteristics of Suitable Criteria**

- **Relevance:** Criteria should contribute to conclusions that assist users in decision-making.
- **Completeness:** Criteria should include all factors that could affect the conclusion.
- **Reliability:** Criteria should allow consistent evaluation of the subject matter.
- **Neutrality:** Criteria should be free from bias and lead to objective conclusions.
- **Understandability:** Criteria should result in clear conclusions that are not open to significantly different interpretations.

Suitable criteria must be available to intended users so they can understand how the subject matter was evaluated.

---
### Evidence

Audit evidence is the information (proof) obtained by the auditor to support the conclusions on which the audit opinion is based.
##### Professional Judgement

Professional judgement is the application of relevant training, knowledge, and experience to make informed decisions appropriate to the circumstances of the audit engagement.  
Professional judgement is essential to the proper conduct of an audit.

Professional Judgement is used when:  
**Applying Standards:** Interpreting and applying ethical requirements and ISAs.  
**Risk Assessment:** Identifying and assessing risks of material misstatement.  
**Audit Planning:** Determining the nature, timing, and extent of audit procedures.  
**Audit Evidence:** Evaluating whether sufficient appropriate audit evidence has been obtained.  
**Forming Conclusions**: Assessing evidence, resolving conflicting information, and evaluating the reasonableness of management's estimates.

Professional judgement is used throughout the audit:  
**Plan → Assess Risks → Gather Evidence → Evaluate Evidence → Conclude**
##### Professional Skepticism

Professional skepticism is an **attitude that includes a questioning mind**, being alert to conditions that may indicate possible misstatement, and critically assessing audit evidence.  
The auditor must perform the audit with professional skepticism, recognizing that conditions may exist that could cause **material misstatement** in the financial statements.

The auditor is alert to:

- Conditions indicating possible fraud or error.
- Contradictory audit evidence.
- Information that questions the reliability of documents or written representations.
- Circumstances requiring additional audit procedures beyond ISA requirements.

**Important Assumption Rule**

- Documents may be accepted as genuine unless there is reason to doubt them.
- The auditor should assume **neither dishonesty nor unquestioned honesty** of management.

**Maintaining professional skepticism** reduces risks of:

- Overlooking unusual or suspicious circumstances.
- Overgeneralizing from limited audit evidence.
- Using inappropriate assumptions when planning or evaluating audit procedures.

**Scepticism = Question everything, but prove nothing without evidence.**

| Professional Judgement              | Professional Scepticism                |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Deciding what to do                 | Questioning what is presented          |
| Uses experience and knowledge       | Uses critical mindset                  |
| Applied in planning and conclusions | Applied throughout evidence evaluation |

The auditor neither assumes that management is dishonest nor assumes that everything is correct. Claims and assertions must be supported by evidence.

**Sufficiency:** The **quantity** of audit evidence obtained.  
**Appropriateness:** The **quality** of audit evidence, determined by its relevance and reliability.
##### Reliability of Audit Evidence

Generally, evidence is more reliable when:

- Obtained from independent external sources.
- Obtained directly by the auditor.
- Documentary evidence is available.
- Evidence is obtained in written form rather than oral form.

**External > Internal**  
**Directly obtained > Client-provided**  
**Documentary > Oral**

Sufficient = Quantity  
Appropriate = Quality  
Appropriate = Relevance + Reliability

This relationship is worth remembering:  
**Audit Opinion ← Evidence ← Sufficiency + Appropriateness**

**Example Question:**
ABC Co has applied for a loan from BG Bank. The bank has asked the directors to provide verified profit and cash flow forecasts along with the loan application. ABC's directors have hired their audit firm to provide an assurance on the forecasts. **What kind of assurance will be provided by the auditors on the profit and cash flow forecasts?**

**Answer:**
Forecasts are based on estimates or judgement of the management. They are also based on events that have not occurred yet. There are only a limited number of procedures that auditors can perform, such as analytical procedures, checking the accuracy of assumptions, and making enquiries of management about how forecasts are prepared. Therefore, the auditors will provide limited assurance with negative wording in report (review engagement).

### No Assurance Engagements

Auditor is not required to give any opinion on these assignments or subject matters.

- **Agreed-upon procedures:** A report on factual findings is given but no assurance expressed. Users must judge for themselves and drawn their own conclusions. This is when the directors hire the audit firm to perform calculations of some task, such as calculation of the depreciation charge for the year or giving the auditor the employees data and payroll charge and ask them to calculate the payroll calculations (without providing them with their own payroll calculations in the first place).
- **Compilation engagement:** Users of the compiled information gain benefit from the accountant’s involvement but no assurance is expressed. It is used to collect, classify and summarize financial information. It means to present data in a manageable and understandable form. The auditors are simply required to structure and format data in an understandable structure for the intended users. 

---
### Inherent Limitations of External Audit

An auditor cannot provide 100% assurance because audits are subject to inherent limitations.

**Incomplete Information:** Management or employees may intentionally or unintentionally fail to provide all relevant information, making it impossible to verify completeness with certainty.
**Fraud and Collusion:** Fraud is deliberate and may be concealed through collusion between individuals, making detection difficult even when appropriate audit procedures are performed.
**Judgement and Evidence:** Audit conclusions are based on professional judgement and persuasive evidence rather than absolute proof.
**Sampling:** Do not test all transactions and balances, only some samples are tested.
**Materiality**
**Subjectivity:** Financial statements include subjective estimates and other judgemental matters.
**Limitations of internal controls:** Internal controls may be relied on which have their own inherent limitations.

---
### Benefits of an Audit

- **H**igher quality information, which is more reliable, improving the reputation of the market.
- **I**ndependent scrutiny and verification may be valuable to management.
- **R**educes the risk of management bias and fraud and error by acting as a deterrent. An audit may also detect bias, fraud or error. 
- **E**nhances the credibility of financial statements e.g. for tax authorities or lenders.
- **D**eficiencies in the internal control system may be highlighted by the auditor.

---
### Expectation Gap

Some users incorrectly believe that an audit provides absolute assurance - that the audit opinion is a guarantee that the financial statements are correct. This and other misconceptions about the role of an auditor are referred to as the "Expectation Gap"
Examples of the expectation gap are:

- A belief that the auditor tests all transactions and balances.
- A belief that the auditor will detect all fraud.
- A belief that the auditor is responsible for preparing the financial statements.

### Maple and Co CR

You are the audit supervisor of Maple & Co and are currently planning the audit of an existing client, Sycamore Science Co (Sycamore), whose year end was 30 April 2015. Sycamore is a pharmaceutical company, which manufactures and supplies a wide range of medical supplies.

Sycamore’s previous finance director left the company in December 2014 after it was discovered that he had been claiming fraudulent expenses from the company for a significant period of time. A new finance director was appointed in January 2015.

Sycamore’s new finance director has read about review engagements and is interested in the possibility of Maple & Co undertaking these in the future. However, she is unsure how these engagements differ from an external audit and how much assurance would be gained from this type of engagement.

**Explain the purpose of review engagements and how these differ from external audits and describe the level of assurance provided by external audits and review engagements - (5 marks)**

**Answer:**

*(3 things are being asked in the question:*
1. *Purpose of review engagements*
2. *Difference between external audit and review engagements*
3. *The level of assurance provided by external audits and review engagements)*

**Review Engagement**
A review engagement is designed to provide limited assurance that financial information is free from material misstatement. Its purpose is to give users a moderate level of confidence without the cost and depth of a full audit. 
In practice, a review:
- Helps stakeholders assess whether anything significant is wrong with the subject matter
- Is commonly used for interim financial statements or non-audit assurance needs
- Relies mainly on analytical procedures and enquiry, rather than detailed testing

**Difference between Review Engagements and External Audit**

| Review Engagement                                              | External Audit                                                                                             |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Provides limited assurance                                     | Provides reasonable assurance                                                                              |
| Lesser testing, comprised of analytical testing and enquiries. | Level of testing is detailed involving substantive procedures, test of controls and analytical procedures. |
| Evidence gathered is limited                                   | Evidence gathered is extensive and persuasive                                                              |
| Depth of work is low to moderate                               | Depth of work in high                                                                                      |
| Outcome - no material issues left                              | Outcome - true and fair opinion of financial statements                                                    |
| Negative wording is used                                       | Positive wording is used                                                                                   |
**Levels of Assurance Provided**
**External Audit:**
- Provides reasonable assurance
- Expressed in a positive form of opinion e.g.
	- "in our opinion the financial statements give (or do not give) a true and fair view of the state of the company's affairs"
- Assurance is high but not absolute or 100% due to inherent limitations of auditing
**Review Engagements:**
- Provides limited assurance
- Expressed in a negative form of conclusion e.g.
	- "Nothing has come to light to suggest that errors or problems exist"
- Assurance is moderate, meaning the practitioner is not saying the information is correct, only that no issues were found based on limited procedures.

### Study Hub Activity 2

**Describe the possible agency relationships between shareholders, directors and auditors.**

**Answer:**
Shareholders are the principals. Directors are appointed as agents of the shareholders to manage the company on their behalf and owe fiduciary duties to the company and its shareholders. Auditors are also appointed by shareholders as their agents to independently examine the financial statements and report whether they give a true and fair view. The auditor's work helps reduce the agency conflict between shareholders and directors.

### Study Hub Activity 3

**Explain the accountability of directors and the auditor to shareholders**

**Answer:**

**Directors:**
Directors are accountable to shareholders for:

- Managing the company.
- Preparing financial statements under the applicable framework (e.g., IFRS).
- Communicating company performance through directors' reports and annual reports.
- Complying with laws, regulations, listing rules, and corporate governance requirements.

**Auditors:**
Auditors are accountable to shareholders for:

- Conducting an independent audit.
- Acting in the interests of shareholders.
- Reporting whether the financial statements give a true and fair view.
- Considering the public interest, although their report is addressed to shareholders, not to other stakeholders.

### Study Hub Activity 4

**State and explain the form of assurance that could be given on a company's code of business ethics**

**Answer**:
This would be a limited assurance engagement. Although there is a Code of Business Ethics, the subjectivity of applying and measuring the application of any specific ethical criteria (e.g. what is not ethical to one business may be considered ethical by another) would not enable the practitioner to reduce assurance risk to a sufficiently low level to allow reasonable assurance to be given.

*(This would be a limited assurance engagement. Even though there is a Code of Business Ethics, it is difficult to judge and measure ethical behavior because it depends on personal and business opinions, and different companies may see the same action differently. Because of this, the practitioner cannot gather strong enough evidence to be highly confident that everything is correct, so they cannot reduce the risk of being wrong to a very low level needed for a reasonable assurance engagement. Therefore, only limited assurance can be given)*

### Hessonite and Co - June 2016

**Explain the five element of an assurance engagement (5 marks) - Test Your Understanding 1**

**Answer:**
The five elements of assurance engagement are as follows:

1. **A three-party relationship:** A three-party relationship includes:
	- **A responsible party (the directors):** the person responsible for preparing the information and assertions.
	- **A practitioner (external auditor):** the professional who will review the subject matter and provide the assurance.
	- **Intended users (shareholders):** These are the people for whom the practitioner prepare the assurance report.
2. **Appropriate subject matter:** Data prepared or assertions made by the responsible party. The subject matter can take many forms such as:
	- Financial performance
	- Non-financial performance e.g. KPIs of efficiency and effectiveness
	- Physical characteristics e.g. capacity of a facility
	- Systems and processes e.g. internal control
	- Behavior e.g. corporate governance
3. **Suitable criteria:** These are the benchmarks used to evaluate or measure the subject matter. Examples are:
	- Financial statements -> IFRS
	- Internal controls -> Internal control framework
	- Compliance -> Applicable laws, regulations or contracts
4. **Sufficient appropriate evidence:** This is the information / proof obtained by the auditor to support the conclusions on which the audit opinion is based.
5. **A written assurance report:** This is a written report which provides an opinion given by the practitioner to the intended users.

### CR 2 - Dec 2010

**Explain the concept of True and Fair presentation (4 marks)**

**Answer:** 
Financial statements are prepared by management to show the financial performance and position of a company. The auditor then expresses an opinion on whether these financial statements give a true and fair view.
Although International Standards on Auditing do not formally define “true and fair,” it is generally understood as follows:

**True**
Information is “true” when it:

- Is factually correct and reflects reality
- Contains no material factual errors
- Is prepared in line with accounting standards and relevant laws
- Has been accurately transferred from accounting records into the financial statements

**Fair**
Information is “fair” when it:

- Is clear, unbiased, and not misleading
- Presents information in a neutral way
- Reflects the substance of transactions, not just their legal form

True and fair presentation means financial statements are both accurate in fact and presented honestly and without bias so that users can rely on them for decision-making.

### Brampton Co CR Case - Dec 2009

You are the senior in charge of the audit of Brampton Co for the year ending 31 January 2010 and are currently planning the year-end audit. Brampton specializes in the production of high quality bread of various kinds.

During the interim audit you noted that, in the present economic down-turn, the company has suffered as its costs are increasing and its prices have been higher than its competitors because of lower production runs. One indicator of the problems facing the company is that it has consistently used a bank overdraft facility to ﬁnance its activities.

At the time of the interim audit you had discussed with company management what actions were being taken to improve the liquidity of the company and you were informed that the company plans to expand its facilities for producing white bread as this line had maintained its market share. The company has asked its bank for a loan to ﬁnance the expansion and also to maintain its working capital generally.

To support its request for a loan, the company has prepared a cash ﬂow forecast for the two years from the end of the reporting period and the internal audit department has reported on the forecast to the board of directors.

However, the bank has said it would like a report from the external auditors to conﬁrm the accuracy of the forecast. Following this request the company has asked you to examine the cash ﬂow forecast and then to report to the bank.

**Explain the kind of assurance you could give in the context of the request by the bank (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
Forecasts are based on management’s estimates, assumptions, and judgments, and relate to future events that have not yet happened, so they are inherently uncertain. Because of this, the auditor cannot obtain strong, conclusive evidence as in an audit.

The procedures available are limited to analytical review, enquiry of management, and checking the reasonableness of assumptions and calculations used in preparing the forecast.
Therefore, the auditor can only provide limited assurance on the cash flow forecast, expressed in a negative form of opinion, indicating that nothing has come to their attention to suggest the forecast is not reasonable.

### Smithson Co CR Case - June 2008

Smithson Co provides scientific services to a wide range of clients. Typical assignments range from testing food for illegal additives to providing forensic analysis on items used to commit crimes to assist law enforcement officers.

The annual audit is nearly complete. As audit senior you have reported to the engagement partner that Smithson is having some financial difficulties. Income has fallen due to the adverse effect of two high-profile court cases, where Smithson’s services to assist the prosecution were found to be in error.

Not only did this provide adverse publicity for Smithson, but a number of clients withdrew their contracts. A senior employee then left Smithson, stating lack of investment in new analysis machines was increasing the risk of incorrect information being provided by the company.

A cash flow forecast prepared internally shows Smithson requiring significant additional cash within the next 12 months to maintain even the current level of services. Smithson’s auditors have been asked to provide a negative assurance report on this forecast.

**In the context of the cash flow forecast, define the term ‘negative assurance’ and explain how this differs from the assurance provided by an audit report on statutory financial statements. (4 marks) - Test Your Understanding 2 (Kaplan)**

**Answer:**
Forecasts are based on management’s estimates, assumptions, and judgments, and relate to future events that have not yet happened, so they are inherently uncertain. Because of this, the auditor cannot obtain strong, conclusive evidence as in an audit. 

Negative assurance, in the context of a cash flow forecast, means that the auditor does not express a positive opinion that the forecast is correct, but instead states that nothing has come to their attention from the work performed that indicates the forecast is not properly prepared or that the assumptions used are unreasonable.

This differs from an audit of statutory financial statements, where the auditor provides reasonable assurance and expresses a positive opinion that the financial statements give a true and fair view, based on extensive testing and sufficient appropriate audit evidence.

The cash flow forecast is based on future events and assumptions, so only limited procedures can be performed, resulting in a lower level of assurance and a negative form of reporting. Financial statements, however, relate to past transactions supported by audit evidence, allowing the auditor to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence to support a positive audit opinion.
